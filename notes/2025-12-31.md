Constructing VLAN's in order to safely serve public applications

A couple weeks ago my studies led me to the point where I was going to serve an application on the public internet. Not wanting to do this without hardening the security on our home network I did a few things.

First, I went through our router settings and upped the security on the firewall, changed the password to something more secure, tried changing the username as well but the router wouldn't let me, and maybe tweaked one other setting that I cannot think of. The router already doesn't pump out wifi and I also made sure if it did, the network wasn't publicly foundable.

Back to the studies, I remembered my mentor mentioning something about VLAN's and how he can view his entire network traffic. I'm still not sure what he does for the latter, but I recall pfsense being a common thing to pop up on job listings, and pfsense was the first thing to come up when I researched VLANs. Never having worked with them before, I dove in.

I got delayed because I didn't have a spare computer to dedicate to pfsense. So I found a cheap business mini-computer that had a minimum of 4gb of RAM, probably twice what it needs. I also learned I needed some sort of managed switch, all I had was an unmanaged one. I never used switches before in my life. The unmanaged was pleasant to use.

I had to uninstall and reinstall operating systems and play merry-go-round for which computer was going to be a server, which one was going to be my firewall, and which my daily driver.

I found out the pfsense maintainer requires a cart purchsae to download the free version. Seeing the writing on the wall for future anti-open source sentiments, I opted to try OPNsense first. I had never installed freebsd or openbsd variants before.

I couldn't get OPNsense to work, and already a week behind on this rabbit hole I bit the bullet on pfSense. I was amused that they look almost identical on their consoles. I forgot to mention, I relearned some of the partitioning commands for making iso's on a usb, mainly fdisk and dd.

I finally got pfsense installed and plugged directly into my laptop. It took a couple hours figuring out how to make the WAN and LAN interfaces to work. I finally got into the gui, and then had to play musical chairs again with my router, my computers, and the ethernet cables so it all sat in my living room well. Having to unplug and plug back in the system let me test its resiliency.

Once I got pfsense to work I had to put a managed switch behind the firewall but before my devices. Once I did that I was able to setup VLANs on the gui for pfsense and the gui for the switch.

I segmented my network how I wanted it and stopped there having spent several hours on the whole ordeal after being delayed a week or two from lack of hardware.
